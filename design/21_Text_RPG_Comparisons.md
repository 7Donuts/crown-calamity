# Text RPG Comparisons

Research focus: browser and mobile text RPGs, plus one adjacent text strategy game. The official pages below document mechanics, help guidance, or developer updates. They do not establish audience size, revenue, retention, or why players choose these games. No community anecdotes are used as prevalence evidence. Pages were reviewed 29 October 2026; dates are included only where the source itself makes them clear.

| Game and fit | What its own source documents | Useful comparison and limit |
|---|---|---|
| **Torn — close browser RPG comparator.** Its official New Player Missions guide says the city can feel overwhelming, then walks players through character details, training, jobs, gear, a first purchase, equipping a weapon, combat, and later systems with highlighted, step-by-step tasks. [New Player Missions](https://wiki.torn.com/wiki/New_Player_Missions); [Missions](https://wiki.torn.com/wiki/Missions). | Teaches through actions in the live game and uses contracts as a further goal structure. | This is a useful onboarding pattern for a dense text RPG. The guide gives no test results showing that missions improve retention. Crown & Calamity already has an authored Mara introduction and minor/major contracts; test whether the sequence is clear before adding more tutorial text. |
| **SimpleMMO — close mobile text RPG comparator.** Its official update log includes fixes for text readability, missing material display, preventing a task reward from being claimed twice, and clearer countdown wording. [Game Updates](https://simple-mmo.com/updates). | Ongoing interface and state-correctness work in a compact text RPG, including reward duplication and progress-display details. | These notes show the kinds of bugs and wording that developers chose to fix; they do not say how often players encountered them or what effect the fixes had. Crown & Calamity already uses readable system text, material source hints, explicit costs, and one-time contract claims; validate those points with players. |
| **Race War Kingdoms — close long-running browser RPG comparator.** Its official FAQ addresses why fighting may appear not to advance character level when experience is being heavily allocated to skills; it also explains a font-size setting and reports of confusing item names after a cached interface. [FAQ](https://rwk1.racewarkingdoms.com/faq.htm). | Character XP and skill XP can compete; text scale and item naming have explicit help entries. | FAQ topics establish that these questions merit explanation, not their frequency. Crown & Calamity’s multipart progress, rewards, and item previews should keep XP allocation and gear changes visible at the point of action. |
| **Avabur — close browser text RPG comparator.** Its official overview lists combat, skills, equipment obtained or made through finding, buying, and crafting, alongside companions and optional clans. [Official overview](https://www.avabur.com/). | A text RPG can expose several progression and social systems around a personal combat loop. | The overview is a feature list, not evidence that players need all systems or that those features cause success. Crown & Calamity should test the value of its individual build and story choices before adding social breadth. |
| **Kings of Chaos — adjacent text strategy game.** Official help describes race selection, specialized attack/defense/spy/sentry ratings, rank thresholds, equipment repair, and time-accumulated turns/income. [Help](https://www.kingsofchaos.com/help.php); [Help v2](https://www.kingsofchaos.com/helpv2.php). | Long-term identity and comparative progression can be expressed through a small set of visible ratings and ranks. | It is an army and time-turn strategy game, not a close active-play RPG comparator. Its passive turn economy conflicts with Crown & Calamity’s explicit-action rule, so only the clear rating/rank presentation is worth testing here. |

None of these sources tests a 650 ms action delay, comfortable repeated combat input, multipart character levels, or classless builds. Those remain Crown & Calamity hypotheses. The game already implements one exchange per explicit action, no offline progress, classless attributes and loadouts, multipart levels, an affordable outfitter, a visible story-contract chain, route choices, and a current-contract summary. The comparisons suggest where to inspect clarity; they do not justify copying another game’s timers or claiming that its mechanics prove ours will work.

## Cheap hypotheses to test

1. **Goal visibility:** after creating a character, ask a new player to state the next short goal and the next major milestone. Record time, taps, and any wrong assumptions using the existing Mara contract card. If they cannot find either goal, test one more prominent progress summary before writing more tutorial text.
2. **First useful purchase:** start with the existing 20 coins and level-1 shop. Ask the player to compare one affordable piece, predict what it changes, then buy or decline it. Record whether they understand the tradeoff, how many hunts they expect before another purchase, and whether the purchase changes their chosen build.
3. **Comfortable repetition:** provide a rat or scavenger target and invite the player to repeat any action for a short session. Do not request action variety. After several encounters, ask what felt rewarding, what felt slow, and whether they wanted to continue or stop. Review the 650 ms pacing and part/coin/contract feedback separately.
4. **Story consequence:** after the explicit Mara rescue, ask who was helped and what changed. At the Municipal Works route choice, ask the player to explain what each route puts at stake before selecting. Record recall and choice reasoning, not whether they chose a preferred branch.
5. **Return orientation:** leave a character midway through a contract and a level part, then reopen the game. Ask the player to find the current objective, part progress, and a sensible next action. Record time to orientation, screen changes, and any mistaken belief that progress was lost.

These are usability and pacing hypotheses, not retention experiments. Compare observed behavior with each participant’s explanation; do not interpret repeated actions, session length, or one route preference as a market signal.
